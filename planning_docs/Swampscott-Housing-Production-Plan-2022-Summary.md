# Swampscott Housing Production Plan (2022): Condensed Reference for Meeting Summaries

**Source:** *Swampscott Housing Production Plan 2022* (file name "2022 Revised"; 102 pages), prepared by Karen Sunnarborg Consulting with technical support from Marzie Galazka (Director of Community and Economic Development) and Marissa Meaney (Land Use Board Coordinator), using some 2016 MAPC housing plan data. It updates the 2016 Housing Production Plan.

**Status caution:** every page header reads **"Draft 11-8-22,"** and the cover and text still contain placeholders ("List members," "Funding provided by ?," "Others?"). This file summarizes that draft. Check whether the Town adopted a later or final version and whether the state approved it, and prefer the later version if one exists.

**Strategy labels used here** (the plan numbers strategies within each group): **Zoning A1-A5**, **Development & Preservation B1-B4**, **Capacity-building C1-C3**. Page references are the plan's printed numbers. The plan says "Board of Selectmen"; that body is now the Select Board.

## How to use this file when summarizing meetings

- Connect a meeting item to this plan only when it clearly matches a strategy or need below, and name the label (e.g., "Zoning A4, wider range of housing types").
- This is a state-defined planning document with 2023-2027 production goals. Do not say a board must follow it. Do not say an affordable unit "counts" toward the Subsidized Housing Inventory (SHI) unless the meeting record or a state source says so.
- Facts here are as of 2022 and early 2023. Several items have since moved (see "Later developments" below).

## 1. Plan purpose and framework

- **Purpose:** document demographic and housing changes and set priority actions. The Town also began updating its Master Plan around the same time (see the Master Plan file).
- **Housing goals carried from the 2016 plan:** (1) create opportunities for a more diverse housing stock, affordable and market-rate; (2) give seniors and people with disabilities more housing options; (3) identify the most appropriate sites for projected housing growth; (4) maintain and advance local capacity and advocacy; (5) identify funding sources and programs; (6) promote healthy housing and living.
- **State Housing Production Program:** a town with an approved plan can seek DHCD certification by producing 0.50% (one year) or 1.0% (two years) of its year-round housing as SHI units. For Swampscott that is **at least 32 affordable units a year (64 over two years)**, based on 6,362 year-round units. With certification, the Zoning Board of Appeals may be able to deny a comprehensive permit (Chapter 40B) application without the developer being able to appeal. All units in a 40B rental development count for the SHI; only 25% count in a homeownership project.
- **Sources of strategies:** 2016 plan, updated needs assessment, a **Community Housing Workshop on August 29, 2022**, the Swampscott for All Ages (SFAA) 2019 Community Needs Assessment and 2021 Action Plan, and practice in comparable communities.

## 2. Housing needs assessment (2020 census and related data)

**People**
- Population **15,111** in 2020 (+9.6% since 2010). The plan notes MAPC's 2030 projection (13,927) appears too low.
- Under-20 share fell from 25.5% to 22.2% (2000-2020); ages 20-34 rose to 15.3% (2,319) in 2020; ages 55-64 rose from 9.7% to 14.3%; age 65+ is 19.2% and projected to reach about 29% by 2030 (from about 18% in 2010).
- 70.3% of households are families. Median household income was **$102,898** (2020); half of households earn $100,000 or more, while 20.9% earn under $35,000. About 17.3% of households (1,052) earn under $25,000. 739 residents (4.9%) are below the poverty line. 1,581 residents (10.5%) report a disability, including 950 seniors.

**Housing stock**
- **6,362 units** in 2020 (+8% since 2010). Vacancy 4.7% overall (1% for homeownership, 6.5% for rentals).
- A net gain of **364 rental units** from 2010 to 2020 (56% of all new units), driven by Elm Place, the Machon School and the Paradise Road rental complex. Owner-occupancy fell to 73.7% (from 77.9% in 2010). 23% of units are in buildings with three or more units.
- About half of units were built before World War II; 79% before 1980 (lead paint, accessibility and system-condition concerns).

**Affordability**
- Median single-family price **$703,333** at end of 2021 (county median $585,500). A median-income household could afford about $388,000 (95% financing), leaving a gap of about **$315,333** ($245,833 with 20% down). For a household at 80% of area median income (AMI), the gap is about $347,333.
- A typical two-bedroom rent of $2,200 requires roughly $96,000 in income; the median renter household earned $55,913.
- Between 2000 and 2020 incomes rose 45% while the median house price rose 81%.
- **40.5% of households (about 2,457-2,497) are cost-burdened** (spending 30% or more of income on housing), above the state (about one-third) and county (36.6%). HUD data show that of 1,492 households at or below 80% AMI, 997 (two-thirds) overspend and 582 (39%) spend more than half their income on housing.

## 3. Subsidized Housing Inventory (as of March 22, 2022, plus pipeline)

- **251 SHI units = 3.95%** of 6,362 units. Adding Elm Place (120 units) gives **371 units (5.8%)**; the gap to the state's 10% goal is **265 units** even with Elm Place.
- **Swampscott Housing Authority (SHA):** 128 rental units at Cherry Street (36, families), Duncan Terrace (40, elderly/disabled), Doherty Circle/Burrill Street (44, elderly/disabled) and Ryan Place (8, hearing impaired). SHA's waitlist exceeds **8,000 applicants** (about 70% families). SHA properties are aging and need major upgrades; the plan notes ARPA and state financing options.
- **Other SHI units:** Bertram House (59 senior rental units, counted fully because it was built through a comprehensive permit, expiring 2038); Ocean Watch (2 ownership units); DDS group homes (19) and DMH group homes (5); **Senior Residences at the Machon** (38 one-bedroom senior units; 30 at or below 60% AMI and 8 with project-based assistance at or below 30% AMI; ZBA comprehensive permit February 2020).
- **Elm Place (WinnDevelopment, near the commuter rail station):** 120 mixed-income rental units via a "friendly 40B" under the Local Initiative Program (LIP): 43 market (36%), 16 workforce at 120% AMI (13%), 12 at 80% AMI (10%), 34 at 60% AMI (28%), 15 at 30% AMI (13%); unit mix 7 studios, 77 one-bedrooms, 24 two-bedrooms, 12 three-bedrooms. All units count toward the SHI.
- **Other pipeline items:** a permitted 20-unit Pine Street rental (2 affordable; property sold, no permits issued as of the plan); Hadley Elementary School reuse (advisory committee exploring, including affordable housing); possible SHA improvement and expansion.

## 4. Priority housing needs (Section III.E)

1. **Rental housing (first priority):** more subsidized rental housing, especially for very low-income households and families; need shown by loss of rentals over decades and the SHA waitlist.
2. **Homeownership range:** starter housing for younger households, downsizing options for empty nesters, and options for households priced out but not eligible under 40B rules.
3. **Special needs (numbering inferred; the plan labels only #1, #2 and #4 explicitly):** housing for seniors and people with disabilities (only 24 special-needs units exist); support services such as a residential services coordinator.
4. **Housing condition:** programs for home improvement, de-leading, accessibility modifications and historic restoration for low- and moderate-income and elderly owners (the plan labels this Priority Need #4).

## 5. Challenges to production (Section IV)

- Only about **19 acres of developable residential land** (about 1% of land area), mostly scattered, so most new housing must be redevelopment, which costs more.
- Zoning: land is 53% A-2, 19% A-1, 13% A-3 (total about 1,650 acres by district). In A-3, single- and two-family are by right; multifamily under eight units needs a special permit and larger multifamily is not allowed even by special permit. Multifamily is allowed in the three business districts.
- Accessory apartments need a special permit (Section 5.11). **Inclusionary zoning (Section 4.8)** requires 10% affordable units in projects of ten or more units. The Vinnin Square 40R district carries its own affordability requirement.
- Natural constraints (wetlands, flood hazards, topography), school capacity (declining enrollment makes capacity unlikely to be a problem) and water/sewer (MWRA supply) are reviewed; the plan flags questions it had not resolved.

## 7. Housing strategies (Section VI, Table I-1)

**A. Zoning (all led by the Planning Board)**
- **A1. Comply with MBTA Communities guidelines (Years 1-2).** Section 3A of the Zoning Act treats Swampscott as a commuter rail community: multifamily districts allowed by right within about a half mile of transit at an average minimum of 15 units per acre, with minimum unit capacity of **15% of housing units (954 units)**. Final guidelines came out August 10, 2022. The Town hired a Harborlight Community Partners consultant and preliminarily identified the **Vinnin Street area**. Interim compliance required an Action Plan to DHCD by **January 31, 2023**; final zoning adoption was due by **December 31, 2024**. Non-compliance cuts eligibility for the Housing Choice Initiative, Local Capital Projects Fund and MassWorks.
- **A2. New Chapter 40R district in the General Glover area (Years 3-5).** The existing 40R district (about 2.3 acres near Vinnin Square, approved May 2014) saw little development. The Town acquired a key waterfront property and was negotiating with the General Glover restaurant owner for mixed-use and multifamily. 40R brings state incentive payments, a $3,000 per-permit density bonus payment and Chapter 40S school-cost protection; adoption now needs only a simple majority.
- **A3. Modify the accessory apartment bylaw (Years 3-5).** Allow apartments by right inside the existing primary structure, consider detached units by special permit, move required inspections from annual to every three years, and consider raising the size cap (to roughly 950 square feet).
- **A4. Allow a wider range of housing types in more areas (Years 3-5).** "Missing middle" housing (duplexes, small multifamily, cottage clusters, attached units, two-family in all residential districts); revisit design standards; the plan notes short-term rental (Airbnb) concerns.
- **A5. Consider changes to inclusionary zoning (Years 3-5).** The existing bylaw offers density bonuses (up to two market units per affordable unit, capped at a 50% increase); the plan floats raising the requirement from **10% to 15%** (as in the Greenwood district) along with other changes, with the Planning Board and Affordable Housing Trust.

**B. Housing development and preservation**
- **B1. Make suitable public property available for affordable housing (Years 1-2; Select Board, Housing Trust, SHA; Table I-1 shows 73 units).** Hadley School and the Blaney School were mentioned; the Trust would do feasibility analysis and issue RFPs.
- **B2. Partner with developers on private property (Years 1-2; Planning Board, Housing Trust; 60 units).** Use "friendly 40B"/LIP, with universal design, visitability and sustainability principles. (The text near the B1/B2 boundary attributes 60 units to B1, so the unit split is unclear in the source.)
- **B3. Support seniors to remain in their homes (Years 3-5; Select Board, Housing Trust).** SFAA survey found housing costs and home maintenance are seniors' top concern; the workshop raised property tax relief ideas.
- **B4. Support efforts to improve the housing stock (Years 3-5; Select Board).** MassHousing home improvement loans, the Home Modification Loan Program (via Community Teamwork), energy programs, and a small repair grant program modeled on Needham's.

**C. Capacity building**
- **C1. Community outreach and education (Years 1-2; Housing Trust):** annual housing forum, fair housing training for Town staff, workshops, website information.
- **C2. Continue to capitalize the Affordable Housing Trust (Years 1-2; Select Board).** The Swampscott Affordable Housing Trust was established in 2017.
- **C3. Adopt the Community Preservation Act (Years 3-5; Select Board):** surcharge funds open space, historic preservation and housing, with state matching funds; the plan includes surcharge cost examples (for example $51.32 a year on a $400,000 home at 1% with no exemption, at the $12.83 tax rate).

## 8. Later developments mentioned elsewhere in the project files

Based on the Master Plan summary: the MBTA Communities overlay was adopted in May 2024; the Community Preservation Act passed in November 2024; Elm Place (120 units) is under construction; the Hadley School reuse is in design development; the Veterans Crossing 40B (41 units) is in ZBA review; the SHI stood at 4.07% (255 units) in the Master Plan. Treat the production goals above as superseded where later records differ.

## 10. Known quirks in the source

- The text gives the number of cost-burdened households as both 2,457 and 2,497 (40.5% either way); the SHI gap of 265 units assumes Elm Place is counted.
- Table V-1's market and affordable columns overlap (parenthetical market units that qualify for the SHI), so the columns do not add to the 224 total.
- Section numbering inside the plan refers to strategies as "VI.A/B/C"; the A1-C3 labels here are this file's shorthand.
